Case study

Referral Rush

An AI concept that compares provider data sources and flags records that do not match, before a rep hits send. The demo is a short game that lets you feel the difference.

Role
Concept, interaction design and build
Format
Playable demo, about 2 minutes, fictional data
Tools
HTML, CSS and JavaScript in a single page, Web Audio for music and sound effects, hosted on Netlify
Links
Live demo
Team
Solo
Timeline
About a week
Reach
Post seen by about 282 colleagues in an internal group
Evidence
Informal feedback from colleagues. No usage analytics.

The problem

One wrong provider address or phone number can bounce a referral and send a member back to the start. The member waits longer for care, the rep has to redo the work, and the provider's office receives a request it cannot act on.

Who it's for

  • The rep sending the referral. They need to know whether a provider record is reliable before they commit the member's time to it.
  • The member. They need the appointment to happen the first time, without extra calls.
  • Provider data teams. Every bad record a rep finds can feed a ranked fix list, so the data improves for the next rep.

What I noticed

On the referral line, one wrong provider address or phone number can bounce a referral and send a member back to the start. Provider details live in more than one data source, and when the sources disagree, the problem usually shows up only after the referral bounces. That delay lands on two people: the member, who waits longer for care, and the rep, who spends time redoing work.

How the demo works

  • Four calls at a fictional Texas medical group that serves Medicare Advantage members. Two calls use today's tools with no flags. A short rhythm bonus round follows, then two calls with AI confidence flags.
  • Each call gives you a member, or an office staff member calling for one, a member snapshot, and three in-network specialists. You either send the referral or call the specialist's office, which costs 4 minutes.
  • Two of the three records are bad in each call, so the problem shows up fast. Some have a wrong address or phone number that you can fix once you confirm it. Others cannot take the referral at all, for example because they left the network, their credentialing lapsed, or their panel is closed.
  • A bad send costs 15 minutes and a member callback. A good send earns points, with bonuses for speed and for streaks.
  • The flags in round 2 compare three sources, the credentialing file, the plan's network roster and the provider directory. Each record shows a level, a score and a one-line reason.
  • The ending compares minutes and callbacks between the two rounds.

Key design decisions

Flag mismatches before the rep hits send

Options considered
Report bad records after a referral bounces, block the send automatically, or flag the record before the rep sends.
What I chose and why
The AI compares provider data sources and flags records that do not match before the referral goes out. A fix before sending costs a moment, while a fix after a bounce can send the member back to the start.
What I gave up
Simplicity. A check before sending needs fresh data from all three sources and adds a decision point to every referral. A report after the fact would be cheaper to build, though it would find problems too late to help the member.

Explain every flag in plain words

Options considered
A score alone, a color alone, or a level with a score and a reason.
What I chose and why
Each record shows a label (Looks good, Verify first or Likely wrong), a score, and a one-line reason such as "Plan roster shows contract ended Sep 30." A number alone gives a rep nothing to act on or to explain to a member, and a plain sentence can be read at a glance on a live call. The levels also use different dot shapes, so color is never the only signal.
What I gave up
Detail and some trust. One line cannot show the full evidence. Showing the reason also invites scrutiny, so the demo includes one deliberate false alarm, where a second phone number turns out to be a scheduling line. Reps learn that a flag is a prompt to check and not a verdict.

Keep the person in charge

Options considered
Block flagged records automatically, correct records automatically, or let the flags advise while the rep decides.
What I chose and why
Flags advise. The rep can send anyway or call the office to confirm. A confirmed address or phone error can be fixed and sent in the same call, and the send button turns off only after the office confirms the specialist cannot take the referral. Data sources can be wrong or stale, and the rep is the one talking to the member. Verifying a flagged record earns points, so the demo rewards calibrated trust, and the ending says that flags only help if you check the yellow and red ones.
What I gave up
Speed and certainty. Automatic blocking would remove the 4-minute call and prevent more bounces, but one false alarm would block a good provider. Leaving the choice with the rep means some reps will ignore a flag and still bounce a referral.

Make the demo a game

Options considered
Static mockups, a slide deck, a clickable prototype, or a short game with stakes.
What I chose and why
A two-minute game with a patience timer, a minutes counter and callbacks. A rep who has lived this problem can feel the difference between guessing and having flags in a couple of minutes, which a slide cannot do.
What I gave up
Realism. The game simplifies a real workflow, and it makes two of every three records bad so the problem shows quickly. A game can also make real consequences feel light, so each bounce ends with a member or office calling back frustrated.

The solution

Built for comfort and access

  • Flag levels use text and different dot shapes, so meaning never depends on color alone.
  • Music and sound effects can each be switched off, and the demo respects the system reduced-motion setting.
  • Results are announced to screen readers, and the rhythm round works with the space bar.
  • Every member, provider, address and phone number is fictional.

Feedback from colleagues

I shared the demo in an internal community group, and the post was seen by about 282 people.

  • What people said: One colleague called the experience eye opening, thanked me for sharing it, and sent it to her team's chat so they could share their results back with her. She then shared her own result, said she moved too fast, and said she loved it.
  • How it spread: She sent it to her team's channel and asked them to share their results back with her.
  • What I took from it: A colleague who moved too fast is the pressure the demo is built to create. The patience timer and speed bonus pull you toward sending quickly, which is what a live call feels like, and the flags are meant to help in that moment.

This is informal feedback from a small number of replies. The view count shows reach and does not show how many people completed the demo.

How I would measure success

These are proposed measures. The demo uses made-up data, so I have not measured them.

  • Share of referrals that bounce, before and after
  • Time to complete a referral
  • How often reps override a flag, and how often they were right to
  • How many flagged records get fixed, and how quickly

What I would do next

  • Run the demo with a small referral team and, with their agreement, record which flags they check and which they ignore.
  • Tune the thresholds so Verify first flags are right more often, which would make rep trust easier to earn.
  • Build the ranked fix list for provider data teams that the ending describes.
  • Take the concept through privacy and compliance review before any real data is used.
  • Known limit: the flags in the demo are scripted to show the experience. No live model runs, so the demo shows the interaction design and says nothing about how accurate a real check would be.

What I learned

A confidence signal only helps when people can see why it says what it says and can overrule it. The most useful moment in the demo is the false alarm, because it teaches reps to treat a flag as a prompt to check. I also learned how much pressure shapes decisions. The speed bonus and patience timer pushed at least one colleague to rush, and she said so. Designing for that pressure matters as much as designing the flag itself.

Receipts

  • The live demo, playable in about 2 minutes
  • The colleague reaction and the follow-up message, shown above with names and pictures blurred

The rough parts

The flags are scripted, so there is no live model and no accuracy to measure. I have not tested whether reps would act differently on a flag. One colleague said it moved too fast, which is the pressure the demo is built to create and also a real limit.

Enterprise RAG Assistant

A small, readable Python pipeline that answers policy questions from a document set using Claude, and points to the numbered source passages behind each answer.

Role
Product thinking and build
Format
Command-line demo, fictional health plan documents
Tools
Python, sentence-transformers (all-MiniLM-L6-v2), a NumPy vector index, Claude through the Anthropic API, pytest
Links
GitHub repository
Team
Solo
Timeline
About a week
Reach
Public repository
Evidence
5 automated tests pass. No evaluation set yet.

The problem

Healthcare operations staff, such as customer service reps and analysts, need policy answers quickly. Plan documents are long, and a fast answer is only useful if the person can check where it came from. An assistant that sounds sure but gives no source is a risk when someone is advising a member.

Who it's for

Operations staff who answer questions about coverage and referral rules during their work, for example whether a behavioral health visit needs a referral, or how long a specialist referral stays valid.

How it works

  • Chunking. Documents split on paragraph and sentence boundaries, with overlap between chunks.
  • Embedding. Chunks become vectors using a local model, so no API key is needed for this step.
  • Retrieval. A simple index finds the closest passages to a question.
  • Answering. The retrieved passages go to Claude with instructions to answer only from them.

Key design decisions

Every answer points to numbered source passages

What I did
Answers reference numbered passages so a person can open the source and verify the claim.
Why
In operations work, trust comes from being able to check. A cited answer lets someone confirm a rule before they repeat it to a member.
What I gave up
Some brevity, and the format of citations is still basic.

Answer only from the documents, and say when the answer is missing

What I did
The prompt tells Claude to use only the retrieved passages and to say when the answer is not in them.
Why
A clear "not in the documents" is safer than a confident guess.
What I gave up
This relies on the prompt, with no numeric confidence threshold behind it. A stronger version would measure retrieval confidence and warn the user.

A retrieve-only mode

What I did
A --retrieve-only option shows what the system found without calling the language model.
Why
When an answer looks wrong, the first question is whether retrieval or generation failed. Separating them makes the problem easier to find, and lets anyone test retrieval without an API key.
What I gave up
It is a developer tool, so everyday users do not see it.

Local embeddings and no external database

What I did
Embeddings run on the machine, and the index is a simple file-backed NumPy store.
Why
It keeps the project small, readable and easy to run, which suits a reference build.
What I gave up
Scale. A production system would need a proper vector database.

Data handling

The sample documents describe a fictional health plan, so no real member or plan data is involved. Embeddings are created locally, while questions and retrieved passages are sent to the Claude API to produce answers. The project makes no compliance claims. A real deployment in healthcare would need a privacy and security review first.

The solution

A real run

This is captured output from the repository itself: the ingest step, a retrieve-only question, and the tests. It used the built-in hashing embedder, the offline fallback, and made no call to Claude. Scores come from that embedder, so they will differ from the default model.

Answer screens

The three screens below show example answers, using the fictional Northwind Health Plan sample documents. They illustrate the behavior described above and are mockups of the terminal, not captured runs, because the answer step needs an API key.

How I would measure success

These are proposed measures. The project does not have an evaluation set yet.

  • Answer accuracy on a set of question and answer pairs written from the documents
  • Share of answers where the cited passage supports the claim
  • How often the assistant correctly says the answer is not in the documents

What I would do next

  • Build the evaluation set of question and answer pairs
  • Add keyword search alongside vector search, so exact codes such as plan status labels match reliably
  • Add a simple web interface with sources shown beside each answer, and a way to flag a wrong answer
  • Support PDF documents
  • Known limit: it reads only text and Markdown files, and answer quality has not been formally measured.

What I learned

Building this taught me that trust in an AI answer depends on what surrounds it. The wording of the answer mattered less than whether I could see the source, whether the assistant admitted when the documents said nothing, and whether I could tell if a bad answer came from retrieval or from generation. A single prompt instruction to say when it does not know changed how safe the tool felt to use. The next step is measurement. Right now I trust it because of how it reads, and I want evidence from a test set.

Haunted Health Lab

A Halloween quiz about healthcare technology and human judgment. Five fictional haunted cases ask one question: can you tell when the algorithm needs a human?

Role
Concept, interaction design and build
Format
Interactive quiz, about 4 minutes, fictional cases
Tools
HTML, CSS, JavaScript single page, Web Audio, hosted on Netlify
Links
Live demo
Team
Solo
Timeline
About a week
Reach
Seen by about 150 and about 1,000 colleagues in two internal groups, plus 5 reactions on the larger post
Evidence
Informal replies from colleagues. No analytics, by design.
The scariest thing in healthcare tech this Halloween is an AI that never says "I'm not sure."

The problem

Conversations about AI, privacy and accessibility in healthcare tend to live in slide decks and policy documents, where most people skim them. The hard part is helping people practice the judgment call: when a system should hand a decision to a human.

Who it's for

Colleagues in a healthcare organization, from frontline staff to leaders, who work alongside technology and are not necessarily technical. I shared it in an internal community group during the Halloween week.

How it plays

  • Five fictional cases about AI, privacy, content design, accessibility and accountability. Each is a made-up product facing a real kind of decision.
  • Evidence to poke at in every case: run a data scan, flip permissions, decode an alert, hunt for a booking button, drag an automation slider. Each one hides a clue.
  • Four realistic options per case, shuffled every play and scored from 0 to 3. After you choose, you see why, plus the strongest approach and the design principle behind it.
  • A secret sixth case unlocks if you find all five clues. Most people miss at least one.
  • A lab profile at the end with a score out of 15 and one of three outcomes, such as Human-First Architect.

Why a game format

A short, playful quiz gets people to engage with serious topics they might skip in a training. Four minutes with the music on is a small ask, and the fictional setting lowers the stakes, so people can disagree with a case and talk about it. It also gives people something to compare, since a named result is easy to post and invites replies.

Key design decisions

Investigate the evidence instead of reading a scenario

What I did
Each case has evidence to poke at, clues to find and specimens to collect.
Why
Noticing the detail that does not add up is the skill the quiz is about. Exploring makes people practice it instead of reading about it.
What I gave up
Speed and a clean read-then-answer flow. People can skip the evidence and answer straight away, so I added a nudge on the feedback screen that tells them that case still has a clue hiding. The evidence is also a simplified illustration, so the automation slider is a toy and not a model of real risk.

A hidden sixth case as a reward for attention

What I did
Finding all five clues unlocks a secret case, and most people miss at least one.
Why
It gives careful players something to chase and creates a reason to replay. It also mirrors the real lesson, since the missed detail is often the one that matters.
What I gave up
Some people will never see it. The sealed case tells them how many clues they found and has a button that takes them to a missed case, but players who stop at the profile leave it unopened.

End with a profile people can compare

What I did
The quiz ends with a lab profile, and I invited people to post theirs and see who got haunted.
Why
A named result is easy to share and starts conversation, which spreads the topic beyond the individual player.
What I gave up
A profile is a simplification, so it cannot capture how someone actually handles a real case.

Fiction and humor around real issues

What I did
Every case is fictional and wrapped in a Halloween theme.
Why
It lets people talk about privacy, accessibility and AI limits without worrying about real data or real colleagues.
What I gave up
Realism. Made-up apps and tidy cases are easier to talk about, and they leave out the messy politics and budgets of a real decision. The four options per case are also cleaner than real life, where nobody labels one answer as best.

What it does and does not do

This is an education and reflection experience built for fun. It does not train anyone on a specific policy, give legal or compliance guidance, or use any real data.

The solution

These are real screens from the live demo, rendered in a browser for this page. The display fonts fall back to system fonts here, so the live site looks a little different.

Built for comfort and access

  • Music is off by default and has one clear button. It is synthesized in the browser, so there are no audio files to load.
  • Reduced motion is respected. Animations and effects turn off for people who ask their device for less movement.
  • Plain, readable type. Body text uses Atkinson Hyperlegible, which was designed for legibility.
  • Screen reader and keyboard support. Progress, clues and results are announced, focus moves to each new heading, and every control works from the keyboard.
  • Meaning never depends on color alone. Every rating has a text label as well as a color.
  • No personal data. Answers stay in the browser, and there are no accounts, tracking or analytics.

Feedback from colleagues

I shared the quiz in two internal community groups in the week of Halloween. The posts were seen by about 150 and about 1,000 people, and I invited everyone to reply with their lab profile.

  • A colleague who played: She wrote that she enjoyed it, learned something new, and got the Human-First Architect profile. She described her own style as playing it safe first while adding technical contributions to fill gaps, and she asked whether I would keep posting quizzes like this in the group.
  • Another colleague who finished it: She replied days later with screenshots of her full specimen shelf (6 of 6) and her lab profile, a perfect score. She chose to post her result back to the group unprompted.
  • Reach: The larger post collected five thumbs-up reactions and several comments.
  • What I took from it: People posted their results and talked about them, and one colleague shared a full 6 of 6 shelf, so the profile and the sixth case both gave people something to show. One colleague asked for more quizzes like it, which tells me short interactive formats land well in that group.

This is informal feedback from a small number of replies. The view counts show reach and do not show how many people played.

How I would measure success

  • Share of players who finish all five cases, which needs the anonymous results above
  • Share who find all five clues and unlock the sixth case
  • Spread of lab profiles, to see whether the cases separate players or tilt toward one result
  • Whether players can name a moment where a system should hand off to a human, in a short follow-up question

What I would do next

  • Add a short follow-up question after the profile, such as asking players to name a moment their own work should hand off to a human
  • Collect anonymous results, with a clear notice, so I can see where players struggle. The demo has no analytics today, so I cannot say how many people finished it.
  • Known limit: it measures awareness. It does not show that anyone will act differently at work.

What I learned

Writing plausible wrong answers was the hardest part. Every option had to sound like something a reasonable team would say, or the quiz turns into spotting the silly one. Having people flip the permissions or decode the alert before choosing worked better than describing the problem, because they feel the issue first and then name it. I also chose not to track anything, which kept the demo private and easy to share, and it means I know people played from their replies and not from numbers. If I build the next version, a light, opt-in way to see where people struggle comes first.

The rough parts

Signal Room

A calm, browser-based interactive that helps people who are wired differently, including neurodivergent people and people who have lived through complex trauma (C-PTSD), make sense of workplace communication, understand how they work best, and share that with colleagues.

Role
Concept, interaction design and build
Format
Interactive, four rooms, fictional scenarios
Tools
HTML, CSS and JavaScript in a single page, Web Audio for generated sounds, canvas and SVG graphics, hosted on Netlify
Links
Live demo
Team
Solo
Timeline
About a week
Reach
New release
Evidence
No user feedback yet. Everything here is design intent.

The problem

Workplace messages carry a lot of unstated meaning. A short note from a manager can read as a warning to one person and as routine to another. People who process communication differently spend extra energy decoding tone, guessing intent and deciding how to respond, and they often have no easy way to tell colleagues how they work best.

Who it's for

  • Neurodivergent people at work. They need a quick, low-pressure way to interpret a message and choose a calm next step.
  • People who have lived through complex trauma (C-PTSD). Many learned to scan every room for signals, and a vague message can set off that habit. The Decoder gives them a calm reading and a next step.
  • Anyone who learned to read every room growing up. The tool says plainly that you do not need a label to use it.

What I noticed

Two needs showed up together. People wanted help reading the message in front of them, and they wanted a way to explain how they work without having to defend it each time. Under both sat a third need, a way to regulate their nervous system and hear that it is okay. You do not always have to read the room.

The four rooms

  • The Decoder. Eight realistic work moments. Each one shows what your nervous system might hear, what it most often means, and a calm next move with a reply you can copy.
  • Your Wiring. Six questions, about two minutes, that lead to one of five work-style profiles, with where you shine, what drains you, what helps, and a line you can borrow.
  • The Manual. Tap-through prompts across six sections build a one-page guide to working with you. You can copy the guide, or copy it as a message to your manager.
  • Stim Mixer. A five-channel sound mixer (rain, ocean, brown noise, soft hum and chimes) with presets, plus a fidget tray with bubble wrap, a spinner, a squishable Lumi and guided breathing.

Key design decisions

Rooms can be visited in any order

What I did
Four self-contained rooms reached from the navigation and the home page, each card carrying a short label such as the number of messages or the minutes it takes.
Why
Someone arriving mid-meeting needs the Decoder, and someone winding down may want the Mixer. Short labels let people choose without guessing the commitment.
What I gave up
A single guided path. A first-time visitor may not know where to start, and someone could skip the room that would help them most. The signal line on the home page nudges people to keep exploring, and I traded away more hand-holding for the freedom to choose.

The same three layers for every message

What I did
Every Decoder scenario shows what your nervous system might hear, what it most often means, and a calm next move with a copyable reply.
Why
A repeated pattern lowers the effort of reading each new case, and the copyable reply gives the person an action instead of only an interpretation.
What I gave up
Some nuance. Real messages vary more than eight examples can show.

Show the body settling

What I did
A heart rate bar sits under each message. It starts high, then eases to 72 bpm once the message is decoded, and Lumi's face shifts from worried to calm.
Why
The problem is physical as much as informational. Seeing the shift gives people a felt sense that the moment is okay, and that you do not always have to read the room.
What I gave up
Precision. The numbers are a designed illustration and not a measurement, so a clear label would help stop anyone reading them as a biometric reading.

Output that leaves the tool

What I did
The Manual turns tapped answers into a one-page guide a person can copy, or send as a message to a manager.
Why
Understanding yourself helps most when colleagues can act on it. The guide moves the conversation from "explain yourself" to "here is what helps."
What I gave up
Control over what happens next. Sharing is the user's call, so the tool cannot shape how a manager responds, and it assumes a workplace safe enough to share in.

Sensory control for the user

What I did
A calm mode turns off animation and glow, and the page also respects the system reduced-motion setting. Ambient sound starts off, and the mixer suggests starting low.
Why
For people with sensory sensitivity, a lively interface can add to the load it is meant to lift. Control stays with the user.
What I gave up
Some of the playfulness, which dials down in calm mode, and extra interface to maintain.

Privacy and limits stated up front

What I did
Nothing a person taps is sent anywhere. The Manual draft and mixer levels stay in the browser. The footer says the experience is educational and does not give medical or mental health advice or a diagnosis, and that every scenario is fictional.
Why
People will only give honest answers about how they work if they trust where those answers go.
What I gave up
Saving progress across devices, and usage data I could learn from.

Tone and visual design

The interface sits on a dark background with gold and orchid accents. Each room has its own pastel color, and the room cards use bold outlines like stickers. Headings are lowercase in a serif display face. Lumi, a small mascot, changes expression across the experience, and a signal line on the home page starts noisy and clears as you explore. The playfulness is deliberate, and calm mode is there for when someone needs less of it.

Why it matters for healthcare teams

Clear communication shapes how care teams hand off information and how members are treated on a call. A workplace that makes it easier for people to say how they work best supports the people who serve members.

The solution

Screens from the live build. Fonts may differ slightly from the published site.

How I would measure success

These are proposed measures. The scenarios are fictional and I have not measured them. The tool sends no data anywhere by design, so measuring these would need an opt-in feedback form.

  • Share of users who finish the Manual
  • Share of users who say the Decoder helped them choose a next step
  • Whether managers who receive a Manual report a useful conversation afterward

What I would do next

  • Add more scenarios drawn from different roles, including patient-facing work
  • Test the Manual with managers, not only employees
  • Known limit: eight fictional scenarios cannot cover how differently real teams communicate.

No one from the audience has used it yet. Calm mode and the other sensory choices are my design judgment and are untested with the people this is for.

What I would change in my own work

Four projects, one honest list. This is what each one proves, what it does not, and the first thing I would test.

Referral Rush

  • It proves: a flag with a plain-language reason is something people can react to in a two-minute game.
  • It does not prove: accuracy, because the flags are scripted and no live model runs.
  • First test: run the comparison on a small set of real-shaped records and see which flags reps would actually trust.

Enterprise RAG Assistant

  • It proves: sources, "not in the documents" answers and a retrieve-only mode make a small assistant easier to check.
  • It does not prove: answer quality. There is no evaluation set yet.
  • First test: write 30 question and answer pairs from the documents and score them.

Haunted Health Lab

  • It proves: a playful format got colleagues to reply and talk about AI judgment.
  • It does not prove: completion or behavior change. There are no analytics and the feedback is a few replies.
  • First test: add anonymous, opt-in counts and a follow-up question after the profile.

Signal Room

  • It proves: a calm, sensory-aware interface can be designed and built.
  • It does not prove: that it helps. No one from the audience has used it yet.
  • First test: five sessions with neurodivergent colleagues, starting with the calm mode and the shareable output.

The pattern

All four are concept and demo work, built alone, with made-up data. The next step for every one of them is the same: put it in front of the people it is for and write down what happened.
